# Hanson on AI x-risk

Robin on his AI x-risk doomer pals:

> The fundamental thing that's going on is: they see that eventually AI would be powerful and that it would be "other"—and that's it! They don't like that.

> They just don't like the idea of an "other" being powerful (that is: an "other" with its own priorities and agendas). There are other more technical glosses given, or arguments supposedly given, but in the end those don't really matter much. So—when is it appropriate to be afraid of a powerful other?

The key sentence here is "They don't like that".

His first reaction is: “fair enough”. As a naturalist, he doesn’t think there’s a deep answer to the question: *should* you like this or that?

> Changes in values over time are not mainly mediated by philosophical arguments or arguments about values.
>
>There is different conditions induce different selection pressures on cultures and the values the cultures represent. Individuals have different cultural practices and some succeed more than others. Humans groups who have more successful cultural practices displace others and people copy the more successful practices that they see. Over time cultural practices change as a result of invention and imitation and diffusion and differential wealth growth, military conquering. Over time, cultural evolution has been this major force that makes humans different than the other animals and that's why things have changed. Cultural eevolution includes values that is different. Groups have varied in their values and what they valued and how. Cultural evolution has selected for some packages of values that beat others. That would be the major force that has made values change over time. That's not primarily driven by value arguments, it's driven by selection.

His second reaction is: "fair enough, but are you sure you know what you like here?" 

> You were already expecting your descendants to be [substantially different]—regardless of AI. And you are pretty different than your ancestors; they couldn't do much about it. You're in control and they aren't. That is going to be true of your descendants.

Robin thinks that AI x-risk concerns come out of a more general fear of change:
 
>My first priority was to try to separate out AI specific disagreements we might have from other more fundamental disagreements about just the nature of change and what we want to do about change. A hypothesis I have is a great many people concern about AI is really just concern about the future. That is, once they realize the future could just get really strange, they're put off and they would like change to be slowed down or stopped until we figure out what to do about that.
>
>I think that's just been true for a long time. I think people 1000 years ago would have not have been too thrilled with what's happened over the last thousand years if only they had been able to be told where it was going. I think in the last century, when people get glimmers of just how strange the future can be, they get scared and want to back off from that and want to limit change. I think that's just the fundamental nature of the future, which is once you realize just how strange it could get, how fast. Most people are scared, if they care at all, about the future of where it might go, and they want generically to shut it down or slow down change. We've seen that with regulating genetic engineering or nuclear energy or the bioconservatives, discussing the future. Just we saw that in the early 20th century with people concerned about where social evolution was going with respect to communism and social and large scale capitalism.
>
>I think this is the repeated pattern. That's why I want to separate this out, is to talk just generically about what you might be concerned about in general about the future from AI in particular.

CLOSING THOUGHTS

Hansonsian impartiality is diff to non naturalistic impartiality. It’s more rooted in acceptance of the way of things, and a partial valuing of the wonder of the whole situation. 

He agrees that we could just try to resist change as long as possible. He just happens not to be attracted to that, and he thinks many others may share this once they get over future shock. He would like to influence people to adopt his POV partly partly because of his (partial) valuing of earth originating life over other civs. He would like our descendents to become grabby, to become a great intergalactic civ.

A related thing is convergence on value. Platonist might expect convergence on values because of truth-seeking. Naturalist might think there are stable equilibria. 



He continues with the argument that he's already described well on Overcoming Bias ([1](https://www.overcomingbias.com/p/ai-risk-convo-synthesis), [2](https://www.overcomingbias.com/p/ai-fear-is-mostly-fear-of-future)).

> Evolution doesn't say you should be hostile to your descendents, even if they are substantially different you. Because in fact over time descendants are different from their ancestors. You were already expecting your descendants to be [substantially different]—regardless of AI. And you are already realizing that you are pretty different than your ancestors—and that they couldn't do much about it and that you're in control and they aren't. You were already assuming that was going to be true of your descendants.I'm just saying "well AI is another kind of your descendants". And it's also true for them that they will get more powerful than you, and that they will change relative to you. And if you think of them as your descendants then you should maybe have a different stance toward their being powerful and different.


When someone says "outcome X would mean that most of the potential value of the future is lost", how are they thinking about value?

> (1) Platonic theory of value: there are eternal, mind-independent truths about what matters, no matter what any particular mind happens to think about it. A civilisation can value the wrong things, and if it does, some of the value of the future is lost. This loss is grounded in something more than me and my friends saying “we don’t like it”.
>
> (2) Naturalistic theory of value: creatures evolve to value things. What they value changes over time, in a process of competition and selection. Value is in the eye of the beholder, and the eyes and the beholders change over time.
